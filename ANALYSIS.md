# Critical Analysis Document for STATS 607 Project 2

This simulation focuses on reproducing Table 2 in the original generalized tetrad test paper. The reproduction result can be found in `results/raw/results_table2.csv`. The results from the reproduced simulation is close to the results presented in Table 2 of the original generalized tetrad test paper.

In my opinion, the simulation conditions are generally fair. In particular, the authors 
not only tested nonlinear data-generating mechanisms, where the proposed generalized tetrad test should work well but the baseline classical tetrad test might not work, but also tested linear data-generating mechanisms, where both generalized tetrad test and classical tetrad test are expected to work well. Moreover, though a little simple, the data-generating mechanism seems general. One thing that might be slightly unfair is that the nonlinear data-generating mechanisms might favor generalized tetrad test with PSMD over generalized tetrad test with GMM, because PSMD is a more flexible estimator of the bridge functions. No other component of the simulation setup seem to be deliberated designed to favor any method. 

Meanwhile, I am currently not aware of any popular methods for testing the independence of four variables conditional on a shared unobserved latent common cause, except the classical tetrad test that is evaluated in the simulation. Therefore, I did not identify any important baselines or predecessors that the paper missed. 

However, I feel that the data-generating mechanisms used in the simulation might be too simple to be realistic. Currently, the data-generating functions are all polynomials. Although this allows for nonlinearity, it does not contain more complicated yet reasonable components, such interaction terms, log terms, or exponentiation. In addition, note that Setting I represents a scenario with linear structural equations and correct null hypothesis, Settings II(a) and II(b) represent a scenario with nonlinear structural equations and correct null hypothesis, and Settings III(a) and III(b) represent a scenario with linear structural equations and incorrect null hypothesis. However, it seems that the scenario where the structural equations are nonlinear and the null hypothesis does not hold is never tested, despite that this scenario is reasonable in the real world.

I would change the following about the simulation to strengthen it:

1. I would try to add two scenarios where the structural equation is nonlinear and the null hypothesis does not hold. As explained previously, such scenarios are possible in the real world, so adding such scenarios would make the simulation study more convincing. 
2. I would try one or two more complicated data-generating mechanism that involves, for example, interaction terms, log terms, and exponentiations. These more complicated data-generating mechanism might be a better approximation of real-world settings, so adding them would make the simulation study more convincing. 
3. I would add an experiment to investigate how the empirical rejection rate varies with $\alpha_2$ and $\beta_2$. The rationale is that since generalized tetrad test claims to work well under nonlinearity, a reasonable question to ask is whether the test remains robust when the nonlinearity becomes increasingly prominent. Recall that $\alpha_2$ and $\beta_2$ are the coefficients of the $U^2$ term in the generating equations of $X$ and $Y$, respectively. Thus, they can be seen as controlling the degree of deviation from the linearity assumption. If there is an experiment where we plot the empirical rejection rate against $\alpha_2$ and $\beta_2$, then we will be able to see whether the degree of deviation from linearity would change the performance of the generalized tetrad test. If the results show that the performance of the generalized tetrad test does not degrade with $\alpha_2$ and $\beta_2$, then readers will be more convinced about the utility and robustness of the test. 

I made my own visualizations because the original paper does not contain visualizations of simulation results. In particular, my visualizations implemented the third point in the previous paragraph. The visualization showed that the generalized tetrad test using PSMD appears to maintain a conservative Type I error rate as we increase the degree of deviation from linearity, which is potentially a selling point that the original paper missed. That said, the generalized tetrad test using GMM has high Type I error rates under some values of $\beta_2$, possibly because of the misspecification of the bridge functions.

One thing in the results that surprised me is that in setting II(b), although the null hypothesis is actually true, generalized tetrad test with GMM had a rejection rate of almost 1 when the sample size is 1000. I expected that the generalized tetrad test with GMM might not perform very well due to the misspecification of the bridge functions. However, I did not thought that the rejection would be as high as nearly 1.
